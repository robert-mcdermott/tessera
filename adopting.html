Adopting Tessera

This guide takes a research organization from nothing to a running Tessera that people sign in to, with its links curated and a public site published. It puts the steps in order and says what to decide at each; the other guides have the details, and this one links to them.

Plan on an afternoon to install and describe the organization, a night for the first sync, and a few weeks of curation, shared out, before the profiles are trustworthy enough to publish.

Rehearse on the demo organization#

Before your own organization, go through the whole life of an installation on the demo: an invented research institute of about 100 people, built in seconds and needing no network. On any machine with uv and git:

git clone https://github.com/robert-mcdermott/tessera.git
cd tessera
uv sync
uv run tessera demo                                    # builds ./demo

Turn on sign-in: in demo/tessera.toml, under [server], set accounts = true. Then add an admin (you are asked for a password of at least 12 characters) and start the app:

uv run tessera --config demo/tessera.toml users add admin --role admin
uv run tessera --config demo/tessera.toml serve

Open http://127.0.0.1:8000 and sign in as admin (if something else already uses port 8000, serve says so: add --port 9000 and open that port instead). Then:

  1. Curate. Open Review queue, and accept or reject a few suggestions (A and R on the keyboard). Each decision is recorded with your name; Decision log lists them.
  2. Make a profile public. Open a person, choose Make it public on About, and look at Preview.
  3. Publish. Stop the app (Ctrl-C) and run uv run tessera --config demo/tessera.toml publish. Open demo/var/public/index.html: the people and groups who chose to be public, and nothing else.

That is the life of an installation in small: data from public sources, decisions by people, and a site built from what they chose. Delete the demo folder when you are done.

Tessera needs no institutional system: its data come from PubMed, iCite, NIH RePORTER, ClinicalTrials.gov, ORCID, MeSH, and Grants.gov. A roster from human resources or a department list helps, but is optional.

Install#

Install on the server as Running it as a service describes: the systemd units or the container in deploy/, a reverse proxy for HTTPS, and [server] accounts = true. Then:

tessera init           # writes tessera.toml and the data home
tessera users add NAME --role admin
tessera doctor         # every check should pass, except what the first sync will fix

(With systemd, run these as the tessera user, with TESSERA_CONFIG set; with containers, through docker compose run --rm tessera.)

Describe your organization#

Tessera finds your organization in public records by its names, so this is the step that matters most. In tessera.toml, fill in [organization] (its name, how it is written in affiliations, on NIH grants, and on ClinicalTrials.gov, and its email domains) and [sources] contact_email. Describing your organization explains each setting and how to check the result. An NCBI API key in the environment (NCBI_API_KEY) makes PubMed three times faster.

Then make it yours:

  • Its look: under Admin → Branding, your name, colors, and logos, checked for contrast as you go (Look and words).
  • Its words: a vocabulary preset for your kind of organization, so groups and roles have the names you use (The words).

Your people and groups#

The first sync builds the directory from NIH grant investigators. Most organizations add more:

  • A roster: tessera people import roster.csv adds the people grants miss, with ORCID iDs, emails, units, titles, and dates (Import a roster).
  • Groups: divisions, programs, centers, cores, and labs, with members and their roles, by hand or from a file (Groups).
  • Accounts: one for each curator and admin, and for researchers who will keep their own profiles, linked to their person (--person per_…). With single sign-on, accounts are made at first sign-in and roles come from the provider (Single sign-on).

The first sync#

tessera sync

It fetches the organization's grants, studies, and papers, finds each person's papers, scores every candidate with its evidence, and builds the search index and the views behind Find experts and Insight. For a few thousand papers it takes minutes; for a large academic medical center, hours of downloading and up to an hour of work after it (Sizing a server). Run it in the background or through tessera-sync.service, and follow it with tessera runs or the Sources page.

Then schedule the nightly sync and backup (the timers in deploy/systemd), and run tessera doctor --production.

The first weeks of curation#

A sync suggests; people decide. The suggestions come with their evidence and a score, and the review queue puts the quickest and most consequential first. A plan that works:

  1. Week one, the strong suggestions. They are almost always right: work through them person by person, accepting a person's whole strong band at once with Accept all strong when it looks right (see The review queue). This alone makes most profiles usable.
  2. Discovered people and duplicates. Promote affiliated authors who belong in the directory, and merge records that are one person (Discovered people, Possible duplicates). Run a sync afterwards, so new people's papers are found.
  3. Weeks two and three, the possible band. Conflicts and ambiguous names come first. Share the work: curators for a group's people, and researchers for themselves (they know their own papers best).
  4. Then, the evidence model. Once a few thousand decisions are recorded, evaluate the learned weights and consider auto-accepting what the model is sure of (Learned weights and auto-accept).

Decisions survive every sync: a rejected paper is never suggested again for that person, and an accepted one stays (What survives a sync).

Groups, pages, and profiles#

  • Managers keep their groups' pages: what the group does, its themes, its openings, and which of its members' papers count as its own (Groups). Each group's Inbox collects what needs their attention, and a weekly digest can email it.
  • Researchers keep their profiles, or name someone to keep them, and build CVs and NIH biosketches from them (Profiles).

The public site#

When profiles and pages are ready, invite people and groups to make theirs public. Nothing is public until they do, and each chooses what their page shows. tessera publish builds the site as plain files in <data home>/public; serve them with your web server (the examples in deploy/proxy do), and publish after each sync (The public site).

Keep it running#

  • Every night, the sync and the backup; copy backups off the machine (Backups).
  • Every week, a glance at Admin → Operations for failed runs and what needs attention, and at the review queue.
  • At each release, an upgrade: the new code, tessera db upgrade, and a restart (Upgrading). From 1.0, what you build on Tessera (scripts on its API, your configuration, links to the public site) keeps working across minor releases (Compatibility).
  • When something is wrong, tessera doctor, the Sources page, and the logs (When something goes wrong).

Getting help#

  • The guides, here and at robert-mcdermott.github.io/tessera.
  • Questions and problems: the repository's issues.
  • A security problem: report it privately, as SECURITY.md describes.
